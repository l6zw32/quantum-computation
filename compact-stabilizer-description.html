The Bell state has four amplitudes, but the two equations XX|ψ⟩=|ψ⟩XX|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle and ZZ|ψ⟩=|ψ⟩ZZ|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle already determine it. We will prove the general principle: certain nn-qubit states are specified by just nn Pauli constraints. Then we can store those constraints instead of the 2n2^n amplitudes.

Which sets of constraints work?

Let g1,…,gng_1,\ldots,g_n be signed Hermitian Pauli strings. Each squares to the identity. Write In=I⊗nI_n=I^{\otimes n}, and let GG be the group of all products of these generators. We require three conditions:

  • Commuting: gjgk=gkgjg_jg_k=g_kg_j for every pair. This lets us impose the constraints together.
  • Consistent: −In∉G-I_n\notin G. Otherwise the constraints would imply −|ψ⟩=|ψ⟩-|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle, impossible for a normalized state.
  • Independent: no nonempty subset of the generators has product InI_n. Equivalently, none is a product of the others, so no equation is redundant.

These definitions use the signs, not just the Pauli letters. Together, consistency and independence rule out every nonempty product equal to either InI_n or −In-I_n.

Compact-description theorem. If nn signed Pauli generators on nn qubits satisfy these three conditions, their common +1+1 eigenspace has dimension one. Thus they specify a unique normalized pure state up to global phase. Such a state is a stabilizer state. [1]

Why the common eigenspace is one-dimensional

A projector onto a subspace keeps the component inside it and removes the perpendicular component. For each generator, define

Πj=In+gj2\Pi_j=\frac{I_n+g_j}{2}

Because gj2=Ing_j^2=I_n, we have Πj2=Πj\Pi_j^2=\Pi_j. Every vector vv splits as v=Πjv+(In−Πj)vv=\Pi_jv+(I_n-\Pi_j)v: gjg_j fixes the first component and negates the second. For complex columns, the inner product is u†vu^\dagger v; perpendicular means this equals zero. Here Πj†=Πj\Pi_j^\dagger=\Pi_j and Πj(In−Πj)=0\Pi_j(I_n-\Pi_j)=0, so the two components are perpendicular. Thus Πj\Pi_j projects onto its +1+1 eigenspace.

The generators commute, so these projectors commute. Their product

Π=∏j=1nIn+gj2\Pi=\prod_{j=1}^n\frac{I_n+g_j}{2}

projects onto the intersection of their ranges. To see this directly, every vector satisfying all the constraints is unchanged by Π\Pi. Conversely, ΠjΠ=Π\Pi_j\Pi=\Pi for each jj, so every vector produced by Π\Pi satisfies every constraint.

The trace is the sum of a matrix’s diagonal entries. The identity tr(AB)=tr(BA)\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA) follows by expanding both sums; it implies that changing basis preserves trace. Choose a basis from a projector’s range (its possible outputs) and kernel (the vectors it sends to zero). Its matrix then has a diagonal 1 for each range vector and a 0 for each kernel vector. Hence its trace equals the dimension of its range.

Expand the product:

Π=2−n∑A⊆{1,…,n}∏j∈Agj.\Pi=2^{-n}\sum_{A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}\prod_{j\in A}g_j.

The empty subset contributes InI_n, whose trace is 2n2^n. Every other term is a signed Pauli string different from ±In\pm I_n, by independence and consistency. It consequently contains at least one XX, YY, or ZZ factor. Each of those matrices has trace zero, and tr(A⊗B)=tr(A)tr(B)\operatorname{tr}(A\otimes B)=\operatorname{tr}(A)\operatorname{tr}(B): the sum of the diagonal entries factors. Hence every nonempty-subset term has trace zero.

Thus tr(Π)=2−n2n=1\operatorname{tr}(\Pi)=2^{-n}2^n=1. The common eigenspace has dimension one, proving the theorem. Its unit vectors differ only by global phase. This argument uses a large matrix to establish correctness; our simulator never needs to construct it.

We will use two consequences later. Independence makes the 2n2^n subset products distinct, so

Π=|ψ⟩⟨ψ|=2−n∑g∈Gg.\Pi=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=2^{-n}\sum_{g\in G}g.

Here the bra ⟨ψ|\langle\psi| is the conjugate transpose of |ψ⟩|\psi\rangle. Their outer product is the projector onto the line spanned by |ψ⟩|\psi\rangle. Also, for unsigned Pauli strings P,QP,Q, the same trace calculation gives tr(PQ)=0\operatorname{tr}(PQ)=0 when P≠QP\ne Q, and 2n2^n when P=QP=Q.

The actual data we store

Encode each Pauli letter using two bits (x,z)(x,z):

Letter (x,z)(x,z)
II (0,0)(0,0)
XX (1,0)(1,0)
ZZ (0,1)(0,1)
YY (1,1)(1,1)

Add one sign bit rr, meaning the whole string is multiplied by (−1)r(-1)^r. A generator occupies 2n+12n+1 bits; nn generators occupy n(2n+1)=O(n2)n(2n+1)=O(n^2) bits. This is an exact description of the state, without storing complex amplitudes. [2]

Multiplying the binary labels uses bitwise XOR, but the sign is not obtained by simply XORing the sign bits. Actual Pauli multiplication can introduce factors ±i\pm i. For example,

(XX)(ZZ)=(X·Z)⊗(X·Z)=(−iY)⊗(−iY)=−YY.\begin{split}(XX)(ZZ)&=(X\cdot Z)\otimes(X\cdot Z)\\&=(-iY)\otimes(-iY)=-YY.\end{split}

An implementation multiplies the local letters, retains their phase factors, and combines those factors with the original signs. Products of commuting Hermitian strings are Hermitian, so the final phase is +1+1 or −1-1, even when intermediate factors are imaginary.

Check your understanding

Can +XX+XX, +ZZ+ZZ, and +YY+YY all stabilize the same nonzero state?

Show answer

No. Their product is −II-II, violating consistency. For the Bell state the third constraint is instead −YY-YY, already implied by the first two.
