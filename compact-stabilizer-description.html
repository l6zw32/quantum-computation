The previous section described the Bell state using two Pauli constraints. We now prove the general principle: suitable nn-qubit states are completely specified by nn generators. The proof explains why storing these constraints loses no information about the state.

Which sets of constraints work?

Let g1,…,gng_1,\ldots,g_n be signed Hermitian Pauli strings on nn qubits. Write In=I⊗nI_n=I^{\otimes n} for the 2n×2n2^n\times2^n identity matrix, and let GG contain all products of the generators. We require:

  • Commuting: gjgk=gkgjg_jg_k=g_kg_j for every pair.
  • Consistent: −In∉G-I_n\notin G. Otherwise the constraints would imply −|ψ⟩=|ψ⟩-|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle, impossible for a normalized state.
  • Independent: no nonempty subset of the generators has product InI_n. Otherwise one generator would be a product of the others, giving a redundant constraint.

Together, consistency and independence exclude every nonempty product equal to either InI_n or −In-I_n. Simply counting nn equations is insufficient: repeated equations add no information.

Compact-description theorem. If nn signed Pauli generators on nn qubits satisfy these conditions, their common +1+1 eigenspace has dimension one. Thus they specify a unique normalized pure state up to global phase, called a stabilizer state. [1]

The common eigenspace means all vectors satisfying gj|v⟩=|v⟩g_j|v\rangle=|v\rangle for every jj. We will construct a matrix that keeps exactly these vectors, then count the dimension of its output space.

Why the common eigenspace is one-dimensional

Step 1: keep the vectors satisfying one constraint

Definition (Orthogonal projector): A projector onto a subspace keeps the component inside it and removes the perpendicular component. For complex columns, the inner product is u†vu^\dagger v; two vectors are perpendicular when this is zero.

For one generator, define

Πj=In+gj2.\Pi_j=\frac{I_n+g_j}{2}.

If gjv=vg_jv=v, then Πjv=v\Pi_jv=v; if gjv=−vg_jv=-v, then Πjv=0\Pi_jv=0. For example, (I+Z)/2(I+Z)/2 sends α|0⟩+β|1⟩\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle to α|0⟩\alpha|0\rangle.

To check every vector, use gj2=Ing_j^2=I_n to obtain Πj2=Πj\Pi_j^2=\Pi_j. Each vv splits as Πjv+(In−Πj)v\Pi_jv+(I_n-\Pi_j)v: gjg_j fixes the first component and negates the second. They are perpendicular because Πj†=Πj\Pi_j^\dagger=\Pi_j and Πj(In−Πj)=0\Pi_j(I_n-\Pi_j)=0. Thus Πj\Pi_j is exactly the projector onto the +1+1 eigenspace.

Step 2: impose all the constraints

The generators commute, so their projectors commute. Their product

Π=∏j=1nIn+gj2\Pi=\prod_{j=1}^n\frac{I_n+g_j}{2}

is also Hermitian and satisfies Π2=Π\Pi^2=\Pi. It keeps every vector satisfying all constraints. Conversely, ΠjΠ=Π\Pi_j\Pi=\Pi for each jj, so every output of Π\Pi satisfies every constraint. Therefore its range, the space of possible outputs, is precisely the common eigenspace.

Step 3: count the surviving dimensions

The trace, written tr(A)\operatorname{tr}(A), is the sum of a matrix's diagonal entries. For a projector it equals the dimension of its range. Here is why: v=Πv+(In−Π)vv=\Pi v+(I_n-\Pi)v splits every vector between the range and the kernel (vectors sent to zero). Their intersection is zero. A basis chosen from these two spaces gives a diagonal 1 for every range dimension and a 0 for every kernel dimension.

This works because trace does not depend on the basis. Expanding both sums gives tr(AB)=tr(BA)\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA). For an invertible change-of-basis matrix SS, this gives

tr(S−1ΠS)=tr(ΠSS−1)=tr(Π).\operatorname{tr}(S^{-1}\Pi S) =\operatorname{tr}(\Pi SS^{-1}) =\operatorname{tr}(\Pi).

Now expand Π\Pi, choosing either InI_n or gjg_j from each factor:

Π=2−n∑A⊆{1,…,n}∏j∈Agj.\Pi=2^{-n}\sum_{A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}\prod_{j\in A}g_j.

The empty subset contributes InI_n, with trace 2n2^n. Products of commuting Hermitian matrices remain Hermitian: (PQ)†=QP=PQ(PQ)^\dagger=QP=PQ. Each other product is therefore a Pauli string with sign ±1\pm1, different from ±In\pm I_n by independence and consistency. It has an XX, YY, or ZZ factor, each with trace zero. Since tr(A⊗B)=tr(A)tr(B)\operatorname{tr}(A\otimes B)=\operatorname{tr}(A)\operatorname{tr}(B) (the diagonal-entry sum factors), every such term has trace zero.

Thus tr(Π)=2−n2n=1\operatorname{tr}(\Pi)=2^{-n}2^n=1. The common eigenspace is a line, proving the theorem. Its normalized vectors differ only by global phase. These large matrices establish correctness; the simulator will never construct them.

Two consequences will justify measurement later. Distinct subsets give distinct products: equality would let us cancel their common factors, contradicting independence. Therefore

Π=|ψ⟩⟨ψ|=2−n∑g∈Gg.\Pi=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=2^{-n}\sum_{g\in G}g.

Here |ψ⟩|\psi\rangle is a normalized vector on that line. Its bra, ⟨ψ|\langle\psi|, is its conjugate transpose; the outer product |ψ⟩⟨ψ||\psi\rangle\langle\psi| sends vv to |ψ⟩(⟨ψ|v)|\psi\rangle(\langle\psi|v), exactly the projection onto the line. Also, for unsigned Pauli strings P,QP,Q, tr(PQ)=0\operatorname{tr}(PQ)=0 if P≠QP\ne Q because their product has a nonidentity factor; if P=QP=Q, then PQ=InPQ=I_n and the trace is 2n2^n.

The actual data we store

Encode each Pauli letter using two bits (x,z)(x,z):

Letter (x,z)(x,z)
II (0,0)(0,0)
XX (1,0)(1,0)
ZZ (0,1)(0,1)
YY (1,1)(1,1)

Add one sign bit rr, meaning the whole string is multiplied by (−1)r(-1)^r. A generator occupies 2n+12n+1 bits; nn generators occupy n(2n+1)=O(n2)n(2n+1)=O(n^2) bits. This is an exact description of the state, without storing complex amplitudes. [2]

Multiplying letters adds their two-bit labels modulo 2, or XORs them. For example, 10+01=1110+01=11: X·ZX\cdot Z has the letter YY. But the table says X·Z=−iYX\cdot Z=-iY. The labels omit the phase. Consequently, simply XORing sign bits is insufficient:

(XX)(ZZ)=(X·Z)⊗(X·Z)=(−iY)⊗(−iY)=−YY.\begin{split}(XX)(ZZ)&=(X\cdot Z)\otimes(X\cdot Z)\\&=(-iY)\otimes(-iY)=-YY.\end{split}

Multiply the local letters and combine their phase factors with the original signs. For commuting Hermitian strings, the final product is Hermitian and its phase is ±1\pm1, even if intermediate factors are imaginary.

Check your understanding

1. Checking consistency

Can +XX+XX, +ZZ+ZZ, and +YY+YY all stabilize the same nonzero state?

Show answer

No. Their product is −II-II, violating consistency. For the Bell state the third constraint is instead −YY-YY, already implied by the first two.

2. Counting independent constraints

On two qubits, do the two generators ZI,ZIZI,ZI specify a unique state?

Show answer

No. They repeat one equation and are not independent: (ZI)(ZI)=II(ZI)(ZI)=II. Every state |0⟩⊗(α|0⟩+β|1⟩)|0\rangle\otimes(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle) satisfies them. Their common eigenspace has dimension two, spanned by |00⟩|00\rangle and |01⟩|01\rangle.

3. Encoding a signed string

Encode −XZ-XZ using a sign bit followed by the two pairs (x,z)(x,z). How many bits would a list of two generators on two qubits use?

Show answer

The encoding is 1;(1,0);(0,1)1;\,(1,0);\,(0,1): one minus-sign bit and two bits per letter. This row uses five bits. Two rows use 2(2·2+1)=102(2\cdot2+1)=10 bits.
