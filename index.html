Overview

This project will introduce quantum computation through stabilizer states and the Gottesman–Knill theorem. The core is scoped to fit within two lectures for students with the mathematical background of 15-455. It begins with classical bits and the definition of a qubit, then develops a proof that Clifford circuits with final computational-basis measurements can be simulated efficiently by a classical randomized algorithm.

The central question is which quantum computations have efficient classical simulations? Stabilizer circuits provide a useful case study: they can create entangled states, yet their structure permits a compact description and efficient simulation. [1] [3]

Prerequisites. Only the background of 15-455 is assumed: familiarity with classical computation, elementary probability, mathematical proofs, and asymptotic notation. No previous knowledge of quantum computing or physics is required. Short reminders will introduce the complex numbers, vectors, and matrix operations used in the examples and proof. Quantum notation, tensor products, and binary row elimination will be explained where they are needed.

Proposed course outline

The seven sections below form a self-contained core planned for 150 minutes of teaching, including the worked examples, theorem proof, and brief checks for understanding. Using two 80-minute lectures as the upper limit leaves 10 minutes for questions and transitions. These are presentation-time estimates.

We will use the Bell state as a recurring example and prove the Gottesman–Knill theorem for Clifford circuits with final computational-basis measurements. The references offer optional further reading; the core will explain the mathematics it needs.

Quantum states and compact descriptions (80 minutes)

  1. From classical bits to qubits (30 minutes)

    We will start with ordinary bits, introduce a qubit, and explain the notation |0⟩, |1⟩, and |ψ⟩. Short reminders about complex numbers, vectors, and matrix operations will support the definitions of amplitudes, normalization, and measurement probabilities. Applying a Hadamard gate twice will provide one worked example of how gates act and how interference distinguishes a superposition from an ordinary random bit.

  2. Two qubits and the Bell-state example (15 minutes)

    We will build two-qubit notation and tensor products from the four two-bit strings, then define controlled-NOT by its action on those strings. Applying H and CNOT to |00⟩ will produce a Bell state, whose amplitudes and measurement probabilities we will calculate. This interactive example will recur throughout the remaining sections.

  3. Pauli operators and stabilizer constraints (20 minutes)

    We will introduce I, X, Y, and Z, signed Pauli strings, and the equation P|ψ⟩ = |ψ⟩. Using the Bell state's XX and ZZ constraints, we will explain commuting operators and products of generators. The discussion will establish the multiplication and sign rules needed for the simulation proof.

  4. Why the state has a compact description (15 minutes)

    We will define independence and consistency of commuting Pauli generators, and prove the lemma that n such independent constraints determine an n-qubit state up to global phase. Encoding each Pauli letter with two bits and retaining the generator signs gives an O(n²)-bit description. This representation will support the gate and measurement updates below.

Efficient classical simulation (70 minutes)

  1. Updating generators under Clifford gates (20 minutes)

    We will derive the rule P ↦ UPU†, explain the phase gate S, and give the local update rules for H, S, and CNOT, including their signs. Representative calculations and the running Bell example will show why the updated generators describe the correct state. Each gate update will be shown to take polynomial time.

  2. Sampling the final measurements (30 minutes)

    We will sample the final computational-basis measurements one qubit at a time. The proof will distinguish deterministic outcomes from fair random outcomes, justify both probabilities, and derive the generator update after each sampled result. A short introduction to binary row elimination will show how to find a required generator product and its sign in polynomial time.

  3. The Gottesman–Knill theorem and its proof (20 minutes)

    We will assemble the previously proved representation and update lemmas into a classical randomized simulator. An induction over the gates and sampled measurements will prove that the full output distribution is correct, followed by a polynomial running-time bound and the O(n²)-bit state-storage bound. A brief closing discussion will explain what this restricted-circuit theorem says about quantum advantage. The theorem statement appears below.

Main theorem

Theorem (Gottesman–Knill, final-measurement form)

Let C be an n-qubit circuit initialized in |0⟩⊗n that applies s gates chosen from Hadamard gates, phase gates S = diag(1, i), and controlled-NOT gates, and then measures all qubits in the computational basis. There is a classical randomized algorithm that samples exactly the joint distribution of these final measurement outcomes in time polynomial in n + s. Its representation of the quantum state uses O(n²) bits. [1] [3]

Planned proof. Sections 3–6 will establish the representation, gate-update, and measurement lemmas. Section 7 will combine them into a complete proof of the stated theorem, including correctness of the joint output distribution and a polynomial running-time bound.

The guarantee concerns this circuit family and efficient sampling of its outcomes. It does not assert that arbitrary quantum circuits have efficient classical simulations.

Example: Preparing a Bell state

Starting from |00⟩, apply a Hadamard gate H to the first qubit and then a controlled-NOT (CNOT) from the first qubit to the second. Select a step to compare the quantum state, its measurement probabilities, and its stabilizer generators. We use the basis order |q₀q₁⟩, with q₀ on the left. [1]

References and suggested reading

These references support the quantum foundations, stabilizer formalism, and simulation proof. Reading the linked chapters, watching the playlist, or studying the advanced paper is optional and is not part of the 150-minute core.

  1. Ronald de Wolf. Quantum Computing: Lecture Notes.

    Main reference. Selected definitions from Chapter 1 and Section 2.1 support the introduction. Sections 20.1–20.3 support the stabilizer representation and proof; Appendix A is available for additional mathematical background. The core does not require reading these chapters in full. Section numbering refers to the August 2026 edition.

  2. Quantum Computation and Information at CMU. Video lecture playlist.

    Optional background. Selected videos offer another explanation of quantum states, circuits, and entanglement. The playlist is supplementary to the self-contained core.

  3. Scott Aaronson and Daniel Gottesman. Improved Simulation of Stabilizer Circuits.

    Optional advanced reading. Sections II–III give further detail about stabilizer simulation. The core uses a straightforward polynomial-time simulator; the paper’s optimized algorithms and additional extensions are outside its scope.
