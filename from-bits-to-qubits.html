Section 1 · Quantum states and compact descriptions

From classical bits to qubits

States, measurement, and a first example of interference

Learning outline On this page Check your understanding References

A quantum computation has three ingredients: a state that stores information, operations that change the state, and a measurement that produces a classical answer. We will build this model for one qubit. By the end, you should be able to read a state vector, calculate measurement probabilities, and explain a simple example of interference.

No quantum computing or physics background is needed. We will introduce the vector and complex-number notation as it appears.

1. Bits and randomness

A classical bit has value 0 or 1. A deterministic operation, such as NOT, changes that value according to a rule: 0 becomes 1, and 1 becomes 0.

We can also prepare a bit randomly. Tossing a fair coin and recording its result gives 0 and 1 with probability ½ each. On each run, the bit has one value; the probability distribution describes the randomness in how it was prepared.

A qubit, short for quantum bit, also gives a single bit when measured in the way we will define below. Its state before measurement, however, requires a different description. That difference will let operations combine information in ways that ordinary probabilities cannot.

2. Describing a qubit

Vectors and the ket notation

A column vector is an ordered list of numbers written vertically. For one qubit, we use two entries and give these two vectors special names:

|0⟩=(10)|1⟩=(01)

The notation | · ⟩ is called a ket; here it simply denotes a vector. The labels 0 and 1 name the two computational basis states. These vectors form a basis because any two-entry vector can be written uniquely as a linear combination of them.

A pure state of one qubit is represented by

|ψ⟩=α|0⟩+β|1⟩=(αβ)

The Greek letter ψ (“psi”) names the state; α (“alpha”) and β (“beta”) are its amplitudes. Multiplying a vector by a number multiplies each entry, and adding vectors adds corresponding entries. Thus the equation is ordinary vector arithmetic. A linear combination of basis states is called a superposition.

We begin with pure states. A preparation that randomly chooses among different pure states is called a mixture; we will compare one with a superposition below without needing additional notation.

Complex numbers and normalization

Amplitudes may be complex numbers. Recall that a complex number has the form a + bi, where a and b are real and i² = −1. Its squared magnitude is

|a+bi|2=a2+b2

For a real number this is its usual square. For example, |−½|² = ¼ and |i/2|² = ¼. In particular, squared magnitudes are always nonnegative, even when amplitudes are negative or complex.

A valid state vector must be normalized:

|α|2+|β|2=1

This says that the vector has length 1. The reason for this condition becomes clear from the measurement rule.

3. Reading a qubit: measurement

A measurement in the computational basis produces one classical outcome, 0 or 1. For the state α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, the Born rule gives

Pr(0)=|α|2Pr(1)=|β|2

Normalization makes these probabilities add to 1. Amplitudes are not probabilities: we first take their squared magnitudes. This measurement rule is part of the quantum model. [1]

Example: from amplitudes to probabilities

|ψ⟩=35|0⟩+45|1⟩

This state is normalized because (3/5)² + (4/5)² = 9/25 + 16/25 = 1. A measurement gives 0 with probability 9/25 and 1 with probability 16/25. It produces a bit, not the pair of amplitudes.

Measurement also specifies the state afterward. If the outcome is 0, the resulting state is |0⟩; if it is 1, the resulting state is |1⟩. We ignore a physically irrelevant common phase, explained below. Immediately repeating the same measurement, with no intervening operation, gives the same outcome with certainty.

To estimate the original probabilities experimentally, prepare the same initial state afresh for each trial and measure once per trial. Repeatedly measuring a single qubit after its first measurement would instead keep returning that first result.

4. Changing a qubit: gates

In our ideal model, a reversible operation on a single qubit is a unitary gate, represented by a 2 × 2 matrix. Applying it means multiplying the matrix by the state vector. The multiplication rule is

(abcd)(αβ)=(aα+bβcα+dβ)

Each output entry is the corresponding matrix row multiplied against the input column. Matrix multiplication is linear: applying a gate to α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ gives α times its action on |0⟩ plus β times its action on |1⟩.

To be unitary, a matrix U must satisfy U†U = I. Here I is the identity matrix, which leaves vectors unchanged. The dagger † means conjugate transpose: swap rows with columns, and replace every complex entry a + bi by a − bi. This condition ensures that the gate preserves normalization and that U† reverses it. Measurement is a separate operation; it is generally not reversible. [1]

Example: the X gate

The quantum version of NOT swaps the two amplitudes:

X=(0110)X(αβ)=(βα)

Thus X|0⟩ = |1⟩ and X|1⟩ = |0⟩. Applying X to the example above gives (4/5)|0⟩ + (3/5)|1⟩, swapping the measurement probabilities.

5. The Hadamard gate and interference

The Hadamard gate, written H, mixes the amplitudes by adding and subtracting them:

H=12(111−1)
H(αβ)=(α+β2α−β2)

In particular, it turns the basis states into two frequently used superpositions:

H|0⟩=|0⟩+|1⟩2=|+⟩
H|1⟩=|0⟩−|1⟩2=|−⟩

Both states give 0 and 1 with probability ½ when measured immediately. Their amplitudes have the same magnitudes, but a different sign. [2]

Worked example: apply H twice

Start in |0⟩. After the first H, the state is |+⟩, so α = β = 1/√2. Apply the matrix rule again. The new amplitude of |0⟩ is

12(12+12)=12+12=1

The new amplitude of |1⟩ is

12(12−12)=12−12=0

So H|+⟩ = |0⟩, and measuring after both gates gives 0 with certainty. There is no measurement between the two gates.

This is interference: contributions to an amplitude can add or cancel before we turn the final amplitudes into probabilities. The |1⟩ contributions cancel exactly. Squaring the intermediate amplitudes too early would discard the signs that make this calculation work.

The same calculation gives H|−⟩ = |1⟩. Hence applying H twice returns either basis state to itself. By linearity it returns every state to itself, so H² = I. Since H is real and symmetric, H† = H; therefore H†H = I, confirming that H is unitary.

6. Why a superposition differs from a random bit

Compare two preparations. In preparation A, start in |0⟩ and apply H to obtain |+⟩. In preparation B, toss a fair coin and prepare |0⟩ or |1⟩ according to the result. Measuring immediately gives a fair bit in either case.

Now apply one additional H before measuring. Preparation A gives 0 with certainty, as we just calculated. For preparation B, the coin selected |0⟩ on half the runs, producing |+⟩ after H; on the other half it selected |1⟩, producing |−⟩. Either branch gives a fair measurement outcome. The law of total probability gives

Pr(0)=12·12+12·12=12
The same initial measurement probabilities can hide different behavior.
PreparationMeasure immediatelyApply H, then measure
A: the pure state |+⟩0 or 1, each with probability ½0 with certainty
B: a fair random choice of |0⟩ or |1⟩0 or 1, each with probability ½0 or 1, each with probability ½

For a superposition, we combine amplitudes within a state and then take squared magnitudes. For a random preparation, we calculate each branch’s probabilities and average them. The computational-basis measurement probabilities alone therefore do not determine a qubit’s behavior under later gates.

Relative phase and global phase

The sign difference between |+⟩ and |−⟩ is an example of relative phase: one amplitude changes relative to the other. H followed by measurement distinguishes these two states perfectly. For complex amplitudes, phase differences can involve unit-magnitude complex factors other than −1. [2]

A global phase multiplies all amplitudes by the same complex number c with |c| = 1. The vectors |ψ⟩ and c|ψ⟩ represent the same physical pure state. Any unitary gate keeps that factor common, and taking squared magnitudes removes it. For example, |+⟩ and −|+⟩ represent the same state, whereas |+⟩ and |−⟩ do not. [1]

7. Check your understanding

Try each question before opening its answer.

1. Check the normalization

Is (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/2 a valid normalized state vector? If not, replace the denominator to normalize it. What are the measurement probabilities?

Show answer: normalization

Its squared magnitudes sum to |1/2|² + |i/2|² = ¼ + ¼ = ½, so it is not normalized. Dividing by √2 instead of 2 gives (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/√2. The probabilities are then ½ for 0 and ½ for 1.

2. Follow a minus sign

Use the Hadamard rule to calculate H|−⟩. Why does it differ from H|+⟩ even though |−⟩ and |+⟩ have the same computational-basis measurement probabilities?

Show answer: relative phase

Substitute α = 1/√2 and β = −1/√2. The new amplitudes are (α + β)/√2 = 0 and (α − β)/√2 = 1, so H|−⟩ = |1⟩. For |+⟩ the amplitudes instead become 1 and 0. Their relative signs determine which contributions cancel.

3. Insert a measurement

Start in |0⟩, apply H, measure in the computational basis, apply H again, and measure again. What is the final distribution? How does it change if you omit the first measurement?

Show answer: an intermediate measurement

The first H makes |+⟩. The first measurement leaves |0⟩ or |1⟩, each with probability ½. The second H makes |+⟩ or |−⟩, so the final outcome is 0 or 1 with probability ½ each. Omitting the first measurement lets the amplitudes interfere: H²|0⟩ = |0⟩, giving 0 with certainty.

What to carry forward

  • A pure qubit state is a normalized vector of two complex amplitudes, considered up to global phase.
  • A computational-basis measurement gives a classical bit, with probabilities equal to squared magnitudes, and leaves the corresponding basis state.
  • Unitary gates transform amplitudes linearly. Interference makes relative phase matter, even when an immediate measurement cannot see it.
