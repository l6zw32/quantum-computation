From bits to state vectors

A classical bit has value 0 or 1. If a fair coin chooses its value, we describe the preparation by probabilities ½ and ½. A qubit also gives a single bit when measured in the computational basis, but its state is described by amplitudes instead.

A column vector is an ordered list written vertically. The two computational basis states are

|0⟩=(10),|1⟩=(01).|0\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad |1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.

The notation |·⟩|\cdot\rangle, called a ket, denotes a vector. A pure state of one qubit is represented by

|ψ⟩=α|0⟩+β|1⟩=(αβ).|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle =\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}.

Here ψ (“psi”) names the state, and α (“alpha”) and β (“beta”) are its amplitudes. Vector addition and multiplication by a number act entry by entry. A linear combination of basis states is a superposition.

Amplitudes may be complex. For real a,ba,b, the complex number a+bia+bi has i2=−1i^2=-1 and squared magnitude |a+bi|2=a2+b2|a+bi|^2=a^2+b^2. For example, |−1/2|2=|i/2|2=1/4|-1/2|^2=|i/2|^2=1/4. A valid state is normalized:

|α|2+|β|2=1.|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1.

Thus its vector has length 1. We will also consider a mixture: a preparation that randomly chooses among pure states. It need not be described by one pure-state vector.

Measurement: amplitudes become probabilities

For α|0⟩+β|1⟩\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle, a computational-basis measurement returns 0 or 1 according to the Born rule:

Pr(0)=|α|2,Pr(1)=|β|2.\Pr(0)=|\alpha|^2,\qquad \Pr(1)=|\beta|^2.

Normalization makes the probabilities sum to 1. The measurement also leaves the qubit in |0⟩|0\rangle or |1⟩|1\rangle, respectively, up to an irrelevant global phase explained below. An immediate repeat gives the same result. To estimate the original probabilities, prepare the original state afresh for each trial. [1]

Example: probabilities

The state 35|0⟩+45|1⟩\frac35|0\rangle+\frac45|1\rangle is normalized because 9/25+16/25=19/25+16/25=1. Measuring it gives 0 with probability 9/259/25 and 1 with probability 16/2516/25. The amplitudes 3/53/5 and 4/54/5 are not the probabilities.

Gates act on amplitudes

A reversible single-qubit gate is a unitary 2×22\times2 matrix. Applying it means matrix–vector multiplication:

(abcd)(αβ)=(aα+bβcα+dβ).\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}a\alpha+b\beta\\c\alpha+d\beta\end{pmatrix}.

Each output entry combines one matrix row with the input column. This action is linear: U(α|0⟩+β|1⟩)=αU|0⟩+βU|1⟩U(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)=\alpha U|0\rangle+\beta U|1\rangle.

Unitarity means U†U=IU^\dagger U=I. The identity II leaves vectors unchanged; the conjugate transpose U†U^\dagger swaps rows and columns and replaces each a+bia+bi by a−bia-bi. Unitarity preserves normalization and makes U†U^\dagger the inverse operation. Measurement is a separate operation and is generally not reversible. [1]

For example, the X gate is the quantum NOT:

X=(0110),X(αβ)=(βα).X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad X\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

It swaps |0⟩|0\rangle and |1⟩|1\rangle, and swaps the amplitudes of any superposition.

The Hadamard gate and interference

The Hadamard gate adds and subtracts amplitudes:

H=12(111−1).H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
H(αβ)=12(α+βα−β).H\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} =\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha+\beta\\\alpha-\beta\end{pmatrix}.

Its action on the basis states defines two useful names:

H|0⟩=|0⟩+|1⟩2=|+⟩,H|0\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}=|+\rangle,
H|1⟩=|0⟩−|1⟩2=|−⟩.H|1\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}=|-\rangle.

Both states give a fair bit if measured immediately. Their different signs matter when another gate is applied. [2]

Example: apply H twice

Start in |0⟩|0\rangle. After one H, α=β=1/2\alpha=\beta=1/\sqrt2. After a second H, the amplitudes become

α+β2=1,α−β2=0.\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sqrt2}=1,\qquad \frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sqrt2}=0.

Therefore H|+⟩=|0⟩H|+\rangle=|0\rangle: the final measurement gives 0 with certainty. There is no measurement between the gates.

This is interference: contributions to amplitudes add or cancel before we take squared magnitudes. Squaring too early would discard the signs. Similarly, H|−⟩=|1⟩H|-\rangle=|1\rangle. By linearity, H2=IH^2=I for every input. Since H†=HH^\dagger=H, this also verifies unitarity.

Superposition, randomness, and phase

Compare preparing |+⟩|+\rangle with tossing a fair coin and preparing |0⟩|0\rangle or |1⟩|1\rangle. An immediate measurement cannot distinguish their output distributions. An additional H can:

Preparation Measure immediately Apply H, then measure
Pure state |+⟩|+\rangle 0 or 1, each with probability ½ 0 with certainty
Fair random choice of |0⟩|0\rangle or |1⟩|1\rangle 0 or 1, each with probability ½ 0 or 1, each with probability ½

For the random preparation, H produces |+⟩|+\rangle or |−⟩|-\rangle depending on the coin. Either branch gives a fair measurement, so Pr(0)=12·12+12·12=12\Pr(0)=\frac12\cdot\frac12+\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac12. A superposition combines amplitudes; a random preparation averages the branches’ probabilities.

The sign difference between |+⟩|+\rangle and |−⟩|-\rangle is a relative phase: one amplitude changes relative to the other. It affects interference. A global phase, by contrast, multiplies every amplitude by the same complex number cc with |c|=1|c|=1. The vectors |ψ⟩|\psi\rangle and c|ψ⟩c|\psi\rangle represent the same physical state: gates preserve the common factor, and squared magnitudes remove it. Thus −|+⟩-|+\rangle represents the same state as |+⟩|+\rangle, but |−⟩|-\rangle does not. [1] [2]

Show answer

The first measurement leaves |0⟩|0\rangle or |1⟩|1\rangle, each with probability ½. The next H produces |+⟩|+\rangle or |−⟩|-\rangle, so the final outcome is also fair. Without the first measurement, interference gives H2|0⟩=|0⟩H^2|0\rangle=|0\rangle, so the final outcome is always 0.

Carry forward: describe pure states with normalized amplitude vectors, transform them with gates, and use squared magnitudes only when computing measurement probabilities.
