From bits to state vectors

A classical bit has value 0 or 1.

Definition (Qubit): A qubit is a quantum system with a two-dimensional complex state space. Its state determines the probabilities of obtaining 0 or 1 when measured in the computational basis. A single measurement produces one bit, not a probability.

Definition (Ket notation): The notation |·⟩|\cdot\rangle, called a ket, denotes a column vector.

Definition (Computational basis): The two standard basis states of a qubit are

|0⟩=(10),|1⟩=(01).|0\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad |1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.

Definition (Pure state and probability amplitudes): A pure state of one qubit is represented by a normalized vector

|ψ⟩=α|0⟩+β|1⟩=(αβ).|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle =\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}.

Here ψ (“psi”) names the state. The complex coefficients α (“alpha”) and β (“beta”) are its probability amplitudes in this basis.

Definition (Superposition): A linear combination of basis states is a superposition. Vector addition and multiplication by a number act entry by entry.

Definition (Magnitude): For a complex number z=a+biz=a+bi, where a,ba,b are real and i2=−1i^2=-1, its magnitude is |z|=a2+b2|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}. Its squared magnitude is |z|2=a2+b2|z|^2=a^2+b^2. For example, |−1/2|2=|i/2|2=1/4|-1/2|^2=|i/2|^2=1/4.

Definition (Normalization): A state vector is normalized when its squared magnitudes sum to 1:

|α|2+|β|2=1.|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1.

Thus its vector has length 1. A valid pure-state vector must be normalized.

Measurement: amplitudes become probabilities

Definition (Computational-basis measurement): This measurement has outcomes labeled 0 and 1, corresponding to the states |0⟩|0\rangle and |1⟩|1\rangle. For α|0⟩+β|1⟩\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle, the Born rule assigns the probabilities

Pr(0)=|α|2,Pr(1)=|β|2.\Pr(0)=|\alpha|^2,\qquad \Pr(1)=|\beta|^2.

The Born rule is a rule of the quantum model, not something derived from normalization. Normalization ensures that its probabilities sum to 1. The measurement leaves the qubit in the observed basis state, so an immediate repeat gives the same result. To estimate the original probabilities, prepare the original state afresh for each trial. [1]

Example: probabilities

The state 35|0⟩+45|1⟩\frac35|0\rangle+\frac45|1\rangle is normalized because 9/25+16/25=19/25+16/25=1. Measuring it gives 0 with probability 9/259/25 and 1 with probability 16/2516/25. The amplitudes 3/53/5 and 4/54/5 are not the probabilities.

Example: which amplitudes give a 50–50 outcome?

Suppose we want a pure qubit state whose computational-basis measurement gives 0 and 1 with probability 1/21/2 each. By the Born rule, we need

|α|2=|β|2=12,|\alpha|^2=|\beta|^2=\frac12,
|α|=|β|=12.|\alpha|=|\beta|=\frac1{\sqrt2}.

This condition comes from the requested 50–50 distribution; normalization alone only requires the two probabilities to sum to 1.

The magnitude bars matter. We can write α2=β2=1/2\alpha^2=\beta^2=1/2 only if the amplitudes are real. For example,

(i2)2=−12,|i2|2=12.\left(\frac{i}{\sqrt2}\right)^2=-\frac12, \qquad \left|\frac{i}{\sqrt2}\right|^2=\frac12.

The choice α=β=1/2\alpha=\beta=1/\sqrt2 works, but so do α=1/2, β=−1/2\alpha=1/\sqrt2,\ \beta=-1/\sqrt2 and α=i/2, β=1/2\alpha=i/\sqrt2,\ \beta=1/\sqrt2.

To describe every possibility, use Euler's formula for a real angle tt:

eit=cost+isint.e^{it}=\cos t+i\sin t.

By the definition of magnitude and the identity cos2t+sin2t=1\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1,

|eit|2=cos2t+sin2t=1,|eit|=1.|e^{it}|^2=\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1, \qquad |e^{it}|=1.

Geometrically, eite^{it} runs around the unit circle in the complex plane. Every complex number of magnitude 1 has this form. Dividing by 2\sqrt2 therefore gives every complex number of magnitude 1/21/\sqrt2, and

|eit2|2=|eit|22=12.\left|\frac{e^{it}}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 =\frac{|e^{it}|^2}{2}=\frac12.

Definition (Phase): For a nonzero complex number z=reitz=r e^{it}, where r=|z|>0r=|z|>0, its phase is the angle tt, considered modulo 2π2\pi.

Thus all pairs of amplitudes giving a 50–50 outcome are

α=eiϕ2,β=eiθ2,\alpha=\frac{e^{i\phi}}{\sqrt2},\qquad \beta=\frac{e^{i\theta}}{\sqrt2},

where ϕ,θ\phi,\theta are real angles. Both phases can vary. We will return to this example after introducing gates and explain why only their difference is physically relevant.

Gates act on amplitudes

Definition (Unitary matrix): A square matrix UU is unitary if U†U=IU^\dagger U=I, where II is the identity matrix.

Definition (Conjugate transpose): U†U^\dagger denotes the transpose of UU with every entry replaced by its complex conjugate: a+bia+bi becomes a−bia-bi.

Definition (Single-qubit gate): A reversible single-qubit gate is represented by a unitary 2×22\times2 matrix. Applying it means matrix–vector multiplication:

(abcd)(αβ)=(aα+bβcα+dβ).\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}a\alpha+b\beta\\c\alpha+d\beta\end{pmatrix}.

Each output entry combines one matrix row with the input column.

Definition (Linearity): An operation is linear if it preserves linear combinations. In particular, U(α|0⟩+β|1⟩)=αU|0⟩+βU|1⟩U(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)=\alpha U|0\rangle+\beta U|1\rangle. Matrix multiplication has this property.

Unitarity preserves normalization and makes U†U^\dagger the inverse operation. Measurement is a separate operation and is generally not reversible. [1]

Definition (X gate): The X gate is the quantum NOT, defined by

X=(0110),X(αβ)=(βα).X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad X\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

It swaps |0⟩|0\rangle and |1⟩|1\rangle, and swaps the amplitudes of any superposition.

The Hadamard gate and interference

Definition (Hadamard gate): The Hadamard gate is the matrix

H=12(111−1).H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

It adds and subtracts amplitudes:

H(αβ)=12(α+βα−β).H\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} =\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha+\beta\\\alpha-\beta\end{pmatrix}.

Definition (Plus and minus states): We use the names |+⟩|+\rangle and |−⟩|-\rangle for

H|0⟩=|0⟩+|1⟩2=|+⟩,H|0\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}=|+\rangle,
H|1⟩=|0⟩−|1⟩2=|−⟩.H|1\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}=|-\rangle.

Both states give a fair bit if measured immediately. Their different signs matter when another gate is applied. [2]

Example: apply H twice

Start in |0⟩|0\rangle. After one H, α=β=1/2\alpha=\beta=1/\sqrt2. After a second H, the amplitudes become

α+β2=1,α−β2=0.\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sqrt2}=1,\qquad \frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sqrt2}=0.

Therefore H|+⟩=|0⟩H|+\rangle=|0\rangle: the final measurement gives 0 with certainty. There is no measurement between the gates.

Definition (Interference): Interference is the addition or cancellation of contributions to an amplitude before we take its squared magnitude. Squaring too early would discard the signs. Similarly, H|−⟩=|1⟩H|-\rangle=|1\rangle. By linearity, H2=IH^2=I for every input. Since H†=HH^\dagger=H, this also verifies unitarity.

Superposition, randomness, and phase

Definition (Mixture): A mixture is a state obtained by randomly choosing among pure-state preparations. In general, one pure-state vector cannot describe the resulting state.

Compare preparing |+⟩|+\rangle with tossing a fair coin and preparing |0⟩|0\rangle or |1⟩|1\rangle. An immediate measurement cannot distinguish their output distributions. An additional H can:

Preparation Measure immediately Apply H, then measure
Pure state |+⟩|+\rangle 0 or 1, each with probability ½ 0 with certainty
Fair random choice of |0⟩|0\rangle or |1⟩|1\rangle 0 or 1, each with probability ½ 0 or 1, each with probability ½

For the random preparation, H produces |+⟩|+\rangle or |−⟩|-\rangle depending on the coin. Either branch gives a fair measurement, so Pr(0)=12·12+12·12=12\Pr(0)=\frac12\cdot\frac12+\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac12. A superposition combines amplitudes; a random preparation averages the branches’ probabilities.

Global and relative phase

Definition (Global phase): A global phase is a common factor c=eiγc=e^{i\gamma} multiplying the entire state vector, where γ\gamma is real and |c|=1|c|=1.

Global-phase property: |ψ⟩|\psi\rangle and c|ψ⟩c|\psi\rangle represent the same physical state. To see why, any sequence of gates preserves the common factor by linearity:

U(c|ψ⟩)=cU|ψ⟩.U(c|\psi\rangle)=c\,U|\psi\rangle.

For each resulting amplitude aa, the measurement probability is unchanged because |ca|2=|c|2|a|2=|a|2|ca|^2=|c|^2|a|^2=|a|^2. Thus a common phase cannot be detected by applying gates and then measuring. [1]

Definition (Relative phase): When both amplitudes are nonzero, write α=|α|eiϕ\alpha=|\alpha|e^{i\phi} and β=|β|eiθ\beta=|\beta|e^{i\theta}. Their relative phase is δ=θ−ϕ\delta=\theta-\phi modulo 2π2\pi.

A global phase adds the same angle to both phases, leaving their difference unchanged. A relative-phase change can affect interference: |+⟩|+\rangle has relative phase 0 and |−⟩|-\rangle has relative phase π\pi, and H turns them into |0⟩|0\rangle and |1⟩|1\rangle, respectively. [2]

Return to the example: why one angle is enough

Our general 50–50 pure-state vector was

|ψ⟩=eiϕ2|0⟩+eiθ2|1⟩.|\psi\rangle=\frac{e^{i\phi}}{\sqrt2}|0\rangle +\frac{e^{i\theta}}{\sqrt2}|1\rangle.

Factor out eiϕe^{i\phi}, using eiθ=eiϕei(θ−ϕ)e^{i\theta}=e^{i\phi}e^{i(\theta-\phi)}:

|ψ⟩=eiϕ|0⟩+ei(θ−ϕ)|1⟩2.|\psi\rangle=e^{i\phi} \frac{|0\rangle+e^{i(\theta-\phi)}|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.

The factor multiplies the whole vector, so it is a global phase. Multiplying by e−iϕe^{-i\phi} gives a representative of the same physical state:

|ψ~⟩=e−iϕ|ψ⟩=|0⟩+eiδ|1⟩2,|\widetilde\psi\rangle=e^{-i\phi}|\psi\rangle =\frac{|0\rangle+e^{i\delta}|1\rangle}{\sqrt2},

where δ=θ−ϕ\delta=\theta-\phi modulo 2π2\pi, chosen in [0,2π)[0,2\pi). This is the general form of a pure qubit state with 50–50 computational-basis probabilities, up to global phase.

The original α\alpha does not have to be 1/21/\sqrt2. We have chosen an equivalent representative with that first amplitude. For example, (i|0⟩+|1⟩)/2(i|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2 becomes (|0⟩−i|1⟩)/2(|0\rangle-i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2 after multiplying the whole vector by −i-i. Both describe the same physical state, with relative phase −π/2-\pi/2 (equivalently, 3π/23\pi/2).

Check your understanding

1. Measurement between gates

Start in |0⟩|0\rangle, apply H, measure, apply H again, then measure again. What is the final distribution? What changes if you omit the first measurement?

Show answer

The first measurement leaves |0⟩|0\rangle or |1⟩|1\rangle, each with probability ½. The next H produces |+⟩|+\rangle or |−⟩|-\rangle, so the final outcome is also fair. Without the first measurement, interference gives H2|0⟩=|0⟩H^2|0\rangle=|0\rangle, so the final outcome is always 0.

2. Complex amplitudes and probabilities

Consider

|ψ⟩=32|0⟩+i2|1⟩.|\psi\rangle=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}|0\rangle+\frac{i}{2}|1\rangle.

Is this state vector normalized? What are its computational-basis measurement probabilities? Does replacing i/2i/2 by −1/2-1/2 change those probabilities?

Show answer

Yes: |3/2|2=3/4|\sqrt3/2|^2=3/4 and |i/2|2=1/4|i/2|^2=1/4, so the squared magnitudes sum to 1. The probabilities are Pr(0)=3/4\Pr(0)=3/4 and Pr(1)=1/4\Pr(1)=1/4.

Replacing i/2i/2 by −1/2-1/2 leaves these probabilities unchanged because |−1/2|2=1/4|-1/2|^2=1/4. It changes the relative phase, but computational-basis measurement alone does not reveal that change.

3. Same probabilities, same state?

Consider the normalized state vectors

|ψ1⟩=|0⟩+i|1⟩2,|ψ2⟩=i|0⟩−|1⟩2,|ψ3⟩=|0⟩−i|1⟩2.\begin{split} |\psi_1\rangle&=\frac{|0\rangle+i|1\rangle}{\sqrt2},\\ |\psi_2\rangle&=\frac{i|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2},\\ |\psi_3\rangle&=\frac{|0\rangle-i|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}. \end{split}

For each, remove any global phase so that the |0⟩|0\rangle amplitude is 1/21/\sqrt2, and identify the relative phase δ∈[0,2π)\delta\in[0,2\pi). Which vectors describe the same physical state? Can computational-basis measurements distinguish these preparations?

Show answer

Since |ψ2⟩=i|ψ1⟩|\psi_2\rangle=i|\psi_1\rangle, the first two vectors describe the same physical state. Multiplying |ψ2⟩|\psi_2\rangle by −i-i gives |ψ1⟩|\psi_1\rangle. Their representative has relative phase δ=π/2\delta=\pi/2, since i=eiπ/2i=e^{i\pi/2}.

The third vector already has first amplitude 1/21/\sqrt2 and has relative phase δ=3π/2\delta=3\pi/2, since −i=ei3π/2-i=e^{i3\pi/2}. It describes a different physical state: a common factor taking |ψ1⟩|\psi_1\rangle to |ψ3⟩|\psi_3\rangle would have to be 1 to preserve the first amplitude, but then it could not change the second amplitude.

All three give Pr(0)=Pr(1)=1/2\Pr(0)=\Pr(1)=1/2, so computational-basis measurements alone cannot distinguish them. Equal probabilities in one basis do not determine the full quantum state.

Carry forward: describe pure states with normalized amplitude vectors, transform them with gates, and use squared magnitudes only when computing measurement probabilities.
