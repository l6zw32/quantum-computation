The generators now describe the state after the circuit's gates. Measurement must produce a bit and update that description. We measure one qubit at a time, so later probabilities can depend on earlier outcomes. This is how the simulator preserves correlations. [1] [2]

From measurement probabilities to signs

Let M=ZjM=Z_j: a ZZ on qubit jj and identities elsewhere. Its eigenvalue m=+1m=+1 corresponds to bit 00; m=−1m=-1 corresponds to bit 11. Thus b=(1−m)/2b=(1-m)/2.

The measurement projector for sign mm is

Qm=I+mM2.Q_m=\frac{I+mM}{2}.

It keeps amplitudes whose jjth bit matches the outcome and zeroes the others. For example, Q+1Q_{+1} keeps terms with bit 00. The Born rule gives the outcome probability pm=‖Qm|ψ⟩‖2p_m=\|Q_m|\psi\rangle\|^2: the sum of those amplitudes' squared magnitudes.

The expectation EE is the average measurement sign, p+1−p−1p_{+1}-p_{-1}. In vector notation,

E=⟨ψ|M|ψ⟩.E=\langle\psi|M|\psi\rangle.

The bra ⟨ψ|\langle\psi| is the conjugate transpose of the column vector. Using Qm†Qm=QmQ_m^\dagger Q_m=Q_m and normalization gives

pm=1+mE2.p_m=\frac{1+mE}{2}.

After an outcome with positive probability, normalize Qm|ψ⟩Q_m|\psi\rangle to obtain the conditional state. We will compute both the probability and new generators without constructing this vector.

Inspect position jj of each generator: II or ZZ commutes with MM; XX or YY anticommutes. There are two cases.

Case 1: a deterministic outcome

Suppose every generator commutes with MM. Then

giM|ψ⟩=Mgi|ψ⟩=M|ψ⟩.g_iM|\psi\rangle=Mg_i|\psi\rangle=M|\psi\rangle.

Thus M|ψ⟩M|\psi\rangle satisfies all the original constraints. Section 4 proved their common +1+1 eigenspace is one-dimensional, so M|ψ⟩=λ|ψ⟩M|\psi\rangle=\lambda|\psi\rangle. Since M2=IM^2=I, λ=±1\lambda=\pm1. The measured sign is certainly λ\lambda, and the state is unchanged.

How can the generator list reveal λ\lambda? Either MM or −M-M is a product of the generators. To prove this, use Section 4's projector onto the state's line:

Π=2−n∑g∈Gg.\Pi=2^{-n}\sum_{g\in G}g.

Here GG contains all generator products. Summing diagonal entries gives tr(MΠ)=λ\operatorname{tr}(M\Pi)=\lambda, hence

λ=2−n∑g∈Gtr(Mg).\lambda=2^{-n}\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{tr}(Mg).

Every term is zero unless g=±Mg=\pm M: a Pauli string containing a nonidentity letter has zero trace. If neither sign occurred, we would have λ=0\lambda=0, a contradiction. This proves existence; the algorithm never enumerates GG.

Find the factors, then recover the sign

Let v(P)v(P) be the 2n2n binary coordinates of PP, ignoring its sign. Recall I↔00I\leftrightarrow00, X↔10X\leftrightarrow10, Z↔01Z\leftrightarrow01, Y↔11Y\leftrightarrow11. Multiplication adds these coordinates modulo 22.

Find bits c1,…,cnc_1,\ldots,c_n satisfying

∑i=1nciv(gi)=v(M).\sum_{i=1}^n c_i v(g_i)=v(M).

Use Gaussian elimination over F2\mathbb F_2, the numbers 0,10,1 with arithmetic modulo 22. Write the 2n2n coordinate equations as rows, including their right-hand sides. Choose an unused row with a 1 in the next variable column as a pivot, swap it into position, and XOR it into other rows with a 1 there; skip a column without a pivot. Repeat, then read off the solution, setting any free variables to 0. These reversible operations preserve the solutions; the product-existence proof guarantees one.

There are O(n)O(n) pivots, each updating O(n)O(n) rows of length O(n)O(n): O(n3)O(n^3) elementary array operations.

Now multiply the selected signed generators using the exact Pauli rules. Their product is εM\varepsilon M, with ε=±1\varepsilon=\pm1: commuting Hermitian generators have a Hermitian product. It fixes the state, so the measured sign is m=εm=\varepsilon. This sign recovery costs O(n2)O(n^2). Binary coordinates find the factors; their signs determine the bit.

Case 2: a fair outcome

Suppose some generator anticommutes with MM; relabel it g1g_1. Because g1g_1 fixes the state and is Hermitian,

E=⟨ψ|g1Mg1|ψ⟩=−⟨ψ|M|ψ⟩=−E.\begin{split} E&=\langle\psi|g_1Mg_1|\psi\rangle\\ &=-\langle\psi|M|\psi\rangle=-E. \end{split}

Therefore E=0E=0, and both signs have probability 1/21/2. Sample one fair sign mm. The normalized conditional state is

|ψm⟩=(I+mM)|ψ⟩2.|\psi_m\rangle=\frac{(I+mM)|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt2}.

Update the generators instead of constructing that vector:

  1. For each i>1i>1 that anticommutes with MM, replace gig_i by g1gig_1g_i, including its sign. Leave other rows unchanged. Use the old g1g_1 throughout.
  2. Replace g1g_1 by mMmM.

Call the other resulting rows h2,…,hnh_2,\ldots,h_n. Each commutes with MM: two anticommutations cancel. Step 1 preserves the old group and independence because each row operation reverses itself.

Every hih_i fixes |ψ⟩|\psi\rangle and commutes with I+mMI+mM, so it fixes |ψm⟩|\psi_m\rangle. The new row mMmM also fixes it, because M2=IM^2=I. All new rows commute: the hih_i are products of commuting old rows and commute with MM.

They remain independent. A relation using only the hih_i contradicts the old independence. A relation involving mMmM would express MM, up to sign, as a product of the hih_i. Such a product commutes with the old g1g_1, whereas MM anticommutes with it: impossible.

They are consistent because the nonzero conditional state satisfies every new constraint. Section 4 therefore guarantees that these nn rows describe exactly the conditional state. At most n−1n-1 length-nn products cost O(n2)O(n^2) elementary array operations.

Example: measure a Bell pair

Start with XX,ZZXX,ZZ. Measuring the left qubit uses M=ZIM=ZI, which anticommutes with XXXX. Sample mm fairly; the new rows are mZI,ZZmZI,ZZ.

For the right qubit, M=IZM=IZ commutes with both. Their product is (mZI)(ZZ)=mIZ(mZI)(ZZ)=mIZ, so its sign is also mm.

First bit Conditional probability that the second bit matches
0 Pr(b1=0∣b0=0)=1\Pr(b_1=0\mid b_0=0)=1
1 Pr(b1=1∣b0=1)=1\Pr(b_1=1\mid b_0=1)=1

Thus 0000 and 1111 each have probability 1/21/2. The second measurement needs no new coin.

Check your understanding

1. A negative generator

The generators are −ZI-ZI and IZIZ. What happens when the left qubit is measured?

Show answer

Here M=ZIM=ZI and −ZI=−M-ZI=-M is a generator. Its sign gives m=−1m=-1, hence bit 11 with certainty. The rows stay unchanged.

2. Updating two anticommuting rows

For a different state, start with generators XX,YYXX,YY. Measure the left qubit and obtain bit 00. What are the new generators?

Show answer

Both rows anticommute with ZIZI. Keep XXXX as the pivot and replace YYYY by (XX)(YY)=−ZZ(XX)(YY)=-ZZ, since (X·Y)⊗(X·Y)=(iZ)⊗(iZ)(X\cdot Y)\otimes(X\cdot Y)=(iZ)\otimes(iZ). Then replace the pivot by +ZI+ZI. The resulting rows are ZI,−ZZZI,-ZZ.

3. Why not use independent coins?

Each Bell-pair bit is individually fair. Would two independent fair coins sample its output correctly?

Show answer

No. Independent coins assign probability 1/41/4 to each string, including the impossible 0101 and 1010. After sampling the first bit, the second must match it. Updating the generators enforces that conditional probability.

Carry forward: sample each measurement from the current generators, then retain the conditional state before measuring the next qubit.
