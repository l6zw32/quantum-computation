The generator list describes the state after the circuit's gates. To produce an output, we must sample its measurements. We will measure qubits one at a time, updating the state after each outcome. These updates preserve correlations between output bits. [1] [2]

A bit value and a sign

Let M=ZjM=Z_j: a ZZ on qubit jj and identities elsewhere. Its eigenvalue is m=+1m=+1 when that qubit is 00, and m=−1m=-1 when it is 11. Thus the output bit is b=(1−m)/2b=(1-m)/2.

The projectors selecting those outcomes are

Qm=I+mM2.Q_m=\frac{I+mM}{2}.

Applied to a state vector, QmQ_m keeps the amplitudes whose jjth bit matches the chosen outcome and zeroes the others. The squared length ‖Qm|ψ⟩‖2\|Q_m|\psi\rangle\|^2 is the sum of their squared magnitudes, hence the outcome probability by the Born rule. Define E=⟨ψ|M|ψ⟩E=\langle\psi|M|\psi\rangle, where the bra ⟨ψ|\langle\psi| is the conjugate transpose of the state vector. Since Qm†Qm=QmQ_m^\dagger Q_m=Q_m,

Pr[m]=1+mE2.\Pr[m]=\frac{1+mE}{2}.

After a possible outcome, normalize Qm|ψ⟩Q_m|\psi\rangle to obtain the new state.

Suppose g1,…,gng_1,\ldots,g_n are our independent commuting stabilizer generators. A generator commutes with ZjZ_j exactly when its letter at position jj is II or ZZ; an XX or YY there means it anticommutes. We can inspect that single column of the generator list.

Case 1: every generator commutes with M

Then M|ψ⟩M|\psi\rangle satisfies all the same constraints:

giM|ψ⟩=Mgi|ψ⟩=M|ψ⟩.g_iM|\psi\rangle=Mg_i|\psi\rangle=M|\psi\rangle.

The common +1+1 eigenspace is one-dimensional, so M|ψ⟩=λ|ψ⟩M|\psi\rangle=\lambda|\psi\rangle. Because M2=IM^2=I, we have λ∈{+1,−1}\lambda\in\{+1,-1\}. The outcome is deterministic, and the state stays unchanged.

To find which sign occurs efficiently, we need a useful fact: either MM or −M-M is a product of the generators.

Here is a proof using the projector from Section 4. Let GG be the set of generator products. Then

Π=|ψ⟩⟨ψ|=2−n∑g∈Gg,\Pi=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi| =2^{-n}\sum_{g\in G}g,
λ=tr(MΠ)=2−n∑g∈Gtr(Mg).\lambda=\operatorname{tr}(M\Pi) =2^{-n}\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{tr}(Mg).

The identity tr(M|ψ⟩⟨ψ|)=⟨ψ|M|ψ⟩\operatorname{tr}(M|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)=\langle\psi|M|\psi\rangle follows by summing diagonal entries. A Pauli string has zero trace unless it is a scalar multiple of II. Thus every summand would vanish unless some g=±Mg=\pm M, contradicting λ=±1\lambda=\pm1. The sum proves existence; our algorithm will never enumerate GG.

Finding the product

Write v(P)=(x0,z0,…,xn−1,zn−1)v(P)=(x_0,z_0,\ldots,x_{n-1},z_{n-1}) for a Pauli string’s 2n2n binary coordinates, ignoring its overall sign. Recall I↔00I\leftrightarrow00, X↔10X\leftrightarrow10, Z↔01Z\leftrightarrow01, and Y↔11Y\leftrightarrow11. Products correspond to adding these vectors modulo 22.

Solve for bits c1,…,cnc_1,\ldots,c_n in

∑i=1nciv(gi)=v(M)over F2.\sum_{i=1}^n c_i v(g_i)=v(M) \quad\text{over }\mathbb F_2.

Here F2={0,1}\mathbb F_2=\{0,1\} uses arithmetic modulo 22. Write the 2n2n equations as rows, including their right-hand sides. In each variable column, choose an unused row with a 1 as a pivot; swap it into the next position. XOR that row into every other row with a 1 in the same column, making those entries zero. Continue with the remaining variables, then read off the solution (set any free variables to 0). These reversible operations are Gaussian elimination over F2\mathbb F_2. The product-existence fact guarantees a solution. There are O(n)O(n) pivots, each updating O(n)O(n) rows of length O(n)O(n), so the cost is O(n3)O(n^3) bit operations.

Now multiply the selected signed generators using the Pauli multiplication rules. Their product is εM\varepsilon M for ε∈{+1,−1}\varepsilon\in\{+1,-1\}. The phase is real because a product of commuting Hermitian generators is Hermitian. Since that product stabilizes |ψ⟩|\psi\rangle, the measured eigenvalue is m=εm=\varepsilon. Binary coordinates find the factors; exact phase arithmetic recovers the answer. Ignoring the signs would lose the distinction between outputs 00 and 11.

Case 2: some generator anticommutes with M

Reorder the generators so that g1M=−Mg1g_1M=-Mg_1. Since g1|ψ⟩=|ψ⟩g_1|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle and g1†=g1g_1^\dagger=g_1,

E=⟨ψ|g1Mg1|ψ⟩=−⟨ψ|M|ψ⟩=−E.\begin{split}E&=\langle\psi|g_1Mg_1|\psi\rangle\\&=-\langle\psi|M|\psi\rangle=-E.\end{split}

Therefore E=0E=0: both outcomes have probability 1/21/2. Sample mm using one fair coin. The corresponding normalized state is

|ψm⟩=(I+mM)|ψ⟩2.|\psi_m\rangle=\frac{(I+mM)|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt2}.

We do not construct this vector. Instead, update its generators:

  1. For each i>1i>1 that anticommutes with MM, replace gig_i by g1gig_1g_i, retaining the exact sign. Leave the other generators unchanged.
  2. Replace g1g_1 by mMmM.

Why is this correct? After step 1, call the other generators h2,…,hnh_2,\ldots,h_n. Each commutes with MM: multiplying two operators that both anticommute with MM cancels the two minus signs. These row operations preserve the generated group and independence, because each operation reverses itself.

Every hih_i stabilizes |ψ⟩|\psi\rangle and commutes with I+mMI+mM, so it also stabilizes |ψm⟩|\psi_m\rangle. The new generator mMmM does too. All the new generators commute.

They are independent as well. A relation using only h2,…,hnh_2,\ldots,h_n would contradict the old independence. A relation involving mMmM would express MM, up to sign, as a product of those hih_i. But all such products commute with the old g1g_1, whereas MM anticommutes with it: impossible.

Finally, the nonzero vector |ψm⟩|\psi_m\rangle satisfies every new constraint, proving consistency. We again have nn independent commuting consistent generators, and hence an exact description of the conditional state.

Worked check: the Bell pair

Start with generators XX,ZZXX,ZZ. Measuring the left qubit means M=ZIM=ZI. It anticommutes with XXXX, so sample m=±1m=\pm1 fairly and update the list to

mZI,ZZ.mZI,\quad ZZ.

For the right qubit, M=IZM=IZ commutes with both generators. Their signed product is (mZI)(ZZ)=mIZ(mZI)(ZZ)=mIZ, so its measured eigenvalue is also mm. The output is therefore 0000 or 1111, each with probability 1/21/2.

Sampling two independent fair bits would incorrectly produce 0101 and 1010. Updating after the first measurement is what preserves the Bell pair's correlation.
